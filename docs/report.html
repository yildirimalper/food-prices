Türkiye · price transmission · September–October 2026

The farm-to-shelf gap

Fruit production rose 60.8% this year and vegetables were flat, yet food inflation is still near 28%. Traders are paying farmers less than last year. So who is collecting the difference?

TurkStat · Eurostat · FAOSTAT · World Bank 409 CPI categories, 2010–2026 Compiled 13 November 2026

Mostly nobody in the food chain. The gap is a cost shock arriving from outside agriculture — and the farmer is absorbing it.

Farm-gate prices rose 12.9% over the year while farm input prices rose 30.3%. That is a 13.3% cut in what a farmer's harvest buys. Retail food, at 26.9%, sits in the middle of the cost ladder — below the minimum wage, below packaging, below rent, below every imported input priced in lira.

The bumper fruit harvest did reach consumers: retail fruit fell 1.0% and was the only deflating category in the basket. It just could not offset meat, vegetables, bread and dairy, which together carry six times fruit's weight.

Food inflation is no longer leading. Housing and utilities ran at 40.2% and transport at 36.4% in October — both well above food. That is the first clue that whatever is driving this is not specific to food.

The farm gate is the slowest-moving price in the economy

Read the distance between the two coloured bars. Everything between them — energy, imported feed and grain, packaging, processing, wages, rent — rose faster than what the farmer received and roughly as fast as what the shopper paid. The chain is passing through a cost shock, not widening a margin.

Where the gap actually opens

This is the single most useful thing in the data for your question. If supermarkets were the ones collecting a rent in 2026, the gap would open at the last step. It does not. It opens at the first handler and processor stage — which is also where the cost of imported feed, grain, fuel and packaging actually lands.

Figure 3

Fruit is the only thing getting cheaper

Had fruit merely tracked the rest of the basket, food inflation would have been 31.3% rather than 28.3%. The harvest was worth roughly 3 percentage points of food inflation to Turkish consumers.

It could not do more, because fresh fruit and vegetables are only 23.5% of the food basket. The other 76.5% is processed food, livestock products and beverages, where the orchard is a minor share of cost and feed, energy, packaging, labour and rent dominate.

Meat at the retail counter is running 9 points ahead of processor prices. Dairy at the processing stage, at 42.5%, is the fastest-rising price anywhere in the domestic food chain — ahead of both raw milk and retail dairy.

Both have independent corroboration. Türkiye's Competition Authority fined BİM, CarrefourSA, Migros, A101 and Şok a combined ₺2.6 billion for Article 4 violations, and has an open investigation covering 50-plus firms and ASÜD across raw-milk purchasing, dairy-feed sales and packaged dairy — including an interim measure over producers being pushed to buy particular feed, a finding that feed-to-milk parity was not honoured, and that payments to producers were delayed to their disadvantage. The data flagged dairy processing before the regulator's docket was consulted. They agree.

In every pair, farm-gate prices rose faster than the matching retail price. Percentage marketing margins narrowed over 2018–2024; they did not widen. Across 2010–2025 the retail-to-farm ratio drifted up only about 25% in total, roughly 1.5% a year, and farmers' real terms of trade actually improved by 9%.

So there is no long-running secular squeeze in the aggregate data. 2026 is a break from the pattern, not a continuation of it — and the break is dominated by one fact: a 60% fruit harvest crashed the farm index in the same year every non-farm cost in the economy rose 25–45%.

A caveat worth keeping

Part of the headline gap is a measurement artefact

Tarım-ÜFE and the food CPI do not measure the same basket. The farm index covers everything Turkish agriculture sells, including cotton, tobacco and exported crops, and it weights fruit heavily — fruit prices collapsed on a record harvest. The food CPI covers what households buy, including imported and heavily processed food, and it weights meat, bread and dairy heavily — all driven by imported feed and energy.

The two indices are moving apart partly because they measure different things. That is why the conclusion here rests on the per-commodity pairs and the stage-by-stage comparisons rather than on the headline "farm +12.9% versus retail +26.9%" alone.

What it adds up to

Five findings

1. The farmer's squeeze is real. Output +12.9%, inputs +30.3%, terms of trade −13.3%.

2. It is mostly not a transfer to traders or supermarkets. Retail food at +26.9% is below the minimum wage, rent, packaging, utilities and every imported input in lira. The gap opens between farm and factory, not factory and shelf.

3. The rent, such as it is, is collected upstream and outside agriculture — by energy, by the exchange rate acting on imported feed and grain, by administered utility prices, by commercial landlords, and by the wage bill.

4. Two genuine margin expansions are visible — meat at retail and dairy at processing. Both are under active competition investigation.

5. Your intuition about fruit was right about the price and wrong about the victim. Traders did pay farmers less, but they passed the lower price on: retail fruit fell 1.0%. The farmer absorbed a normal supply-driven price collapse while their input bill rose 30%. That is the injustice in the data, and it is a terms-of-trade story rather than a margin story.
